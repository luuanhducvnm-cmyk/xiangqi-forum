Piece values are a **rule of thumb**, not a rule of the game. They tell you whether a trade is probably good or bad, but they never replace checking the actual position. Below is a chart that most strong players would call reasonable, and some guidance about when it misleads you.

## The chart

| Piece | Approximate value | Why |
|---|---|---|
| [Chariot](/xiangqi/pieces/chariot/) (車/俥) | **9** | Controls the most points and works on any open file or rank |
| [Cannon](/xiangqi/pieces/cannon/) (炮/砲) | **4½** | Powerful with screens; weaker when the board is open |
| [Horse](/xiangqi/pieces/horse/) (馬/傌) | **4** | Strong in the centre; can be hobbled |
| [Advisor](/xiangqi/pieces/advisor/) (仕/士) | **2** | Defensive, confined to the palace |
| [Elephant](/xiangqi/pieces/elephant/) (相/象) | **2** | Defensive, never crosses the river |
| [Soldier](/xiangqi/pieces/soldier/) (兵/卒), before the river | **1** | Moves only forward |
| [Soldier](/xiangqi/pieces/soldier/) (兵/卒), after the river | **2** | Can also move sideways, and attacks the palace |
| [General](/xiangqi/pieces/general/) (帥/將) | — | Cannot be traded; can help in the endgame |

Using these numbers, a chariot (9) is roughly as valuable as a horse and a cannon together (4 + 4½ = 8½), and about nine soldiers.

## Trade examples

- **Chariot for horse + cannon:** roughly even (9 vs 8½). Which side is better depends on who has the initiative and how safe each general is.
- **Chariot for horse:** a loss of about 5 points. Avoid it unless it wins a decisive attack.
- **Horse for cannon:** about even. In crowded middlegames many players prefer to keep their cannons and trade horses; in endgames the reverse is often true ([cannon page](/xiangqi/pieces/cannon/)).
- **Two defenders for a chariot:** an advisor and an elephant (about 4) for a chariot (9) is a bad trade unless it exposes the enemy general.

## When the numbers lie

Material counts mislead in the following common situations:

1. **Attack on the general.** A player with fewer points but several pieces near the enemy palace can have a winning attack. Sacrifices that lead to a forced [checkmate](/xiangqi/checkmate-patterns/) are worth any number of points.
2. **Defenders still at home.** A side that has both advisors and both elephants is very hard to mate, even when it is behind in material. A lone attacker rarely checkmates a complete palace.
3. **Hobbled or trapped pieces.** A horse whose legs are blocked, or a cannon with no screen and no target, is worth much less than its listed value.
4. **Tempo.** Having the initiative can be worth more than a small material edge, because every threat you make forces the opponent to respond ([strategy](/xiangqi/strategy/)).
5. **Endgames with few pieces.** In sparse positions the horse tends to gain in value and the cannon to lose, because the cannon has too few screens.

## How to use the chart in your games

- **Before a trade,** add up what each side gives and gets. If it is roughly even, decide by position instead.
- **When ahead in material,** trade pieces, especially chariots, to simplify. When behind, avoid trades and look for complications.
- **Count soldiers carefully.** A soldier that has crossed the river and is next to the enemy palace is worth much more than 2 when it supports an attack.
