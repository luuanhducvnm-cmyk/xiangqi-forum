In chess, three-fold repetition is a draw, and perpetual check is a standard way to save a lost game. **Xiangqi works differently.** Because the attacking power of the chariot and cannon is so large, repeating moves to force a draw would be too easy, so xiangqi rules restrict it.

> **Rules differ.** The World Xiangqi Federation (WXF), the Chinese federation and various Asian associations use slightly different wording, especially about chasing. This page gives the common ideas and then explains the simplified rule used by Xiangqi 3D. For an official tournament, read that tournament's rulebook.

## Perpetual check (長將)

A **perpetual check** means giving check on every move so that positions repeat. In xiangqi the side giving the check is **not allowed to continue**: it has to vary its play (for example, give up the attack) or it loses the game. The defender, meanwhile, is simply escaping check and is not at fault.

This is the opposite of chess, where a player in trouble can often force a draw by perpetual check.

## Perpetual chase (長捉)

A **perpetual chase** means threatening the same piece (or pieces) over and over, so that the opponent must keep moving it away and the position repeats. Most official rules also forbid it, but the details are complex: what counts as "chasing", which pieces are covered, and which exceptions apply. For example, many rule sets do not treat attacks on soldiers or on protected pieces the same way as attacks on valuable unprotected ones.

## Repetition with no one at fault

If neither side is giving perpetual check or chasing, a repeated position is simply a **draw**. This also applies when **both** sides are committing a violation at the same time.

## The no-progress rule

If a long time passes with no captures, the game is drawn. The commonly used limit is **60 moves for each side**, or 120 plies, without a capture. Like repetition, the exact wording differs between rule sets.

## How Xiangqi 3D applies it

Xiangqi 3D uses a simplified, consistent version of these rules, applied automatically:

- When the same position has occurred **three times**, the game is judged.
- If **exactly one side** was giving check, or chasing a chariot, horse or cannon, throughout the repeated sequence, that side **loses**.
- If **both** sides or **neither** side were at fault, the game is a **draw**.
- After **60 moves per side without a capture**, the game is a draw.

The "chase" check in the game counts threats against chariots, horses and cannons that are unprotected or are worth more than the attacking piece. It is a practical approximation of the official chasing rules, not a replacement for them.

## Practical advice

1. **Do not rely on perpetual check to save a game.** In xiangqi it usually loses.
2. **If your opponent keeps chasing you,** vary your defence so you are not the one repeating.
3. **Look for a real plan** when a position starts repeating: a different piece, a different file, an exchange.
4. In the endgame, remember the [flying general rule](/xiangqi/flying-general-rule/): sometimes a check is not enough.
