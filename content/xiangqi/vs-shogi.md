Xiangqi, shogi and chess are the three most widely played chess-family games in the world. People often mix them up, so here is a clear comparison.

## At a glance

| | Chess | Xiangqi | Shogi |
|---|---|---|---|
| Country | Europe (globally played) | China | Japan |
| Board | 8×8 squares | 9×10 points; pieces on intersections | 9×9 squares |
| Pieces per side | 16 | 16 | 20 |
| Piece identification | Different shapes | Round discs with Chinese characters | Wedge-shaped, one colour, orientation shows owner |
| Captured pieces | Removed | Removed | Kept by the capturer, who can **drop** them back as their own |
| Promotion | Pawn promotes at the last rank | None | Most pieces promote in the last three ranks |
| Special rule | Castling, en passant | River, palace, flying general | Drops; repetition rules |
| Typical game length | Medium | Medium | Long |

## What stays the same

All three games are two-player, turn-based, deterministic and ask you to attack and ultimately trap the enemy king (or general). In each one you have to develop pieces, coordinate attacks and protect your king, so tactical vision carries over from one to the other.

## Where xiangqi differs from chess

Xiangqi has a river, a palace for the general, a cannon that captures by jumping, and horses that can be blocked. There is no castling, en passant or promotion, and stalemate is a loss. See [xiangqi vs chess](/xiangqi/vs-chess/) for a piece-by-piece comparison.

## Where shogi differs from both

The big idea in shogi is the **drop rule**: a captured piece joins your reserve, and on a later turn you can place it on any suitable empty square instead of moving. This makes material much more dynamic than in chess or xiangqi, because pieces never leave the game. Most shogi pieces also promote when they reach the far side of the board, which makes promotion a constant tactical theme.

## Which one should I learn?

- **If you want the quickest start:** xiangqi or chess. Both have 16 pieces, and the rules fit on one page ([how to play xiangqi](/xiangqi/how-to-play/), [how to play chess](/chess/how-to-play/)).
- **If you already play chess:** xiangqi is the most natural next step. The chariot is a rook, and the new ideas are the cannon, the river and the palace.
- **If you enjoy puzzles about material and tempo:** shogi's drops give a very different feel, but you will need a book or a teacher in Japanese or English.
