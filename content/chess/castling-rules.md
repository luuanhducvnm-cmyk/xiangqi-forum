**Castling** is the only move in chess where you move two pieces at once. It tucks your king away from the centre and brings a rook into the game, which is why most players castle within the first ten moves.

## How castling works

- **Kingside castling (short)**: the king moves two squares toward the h-file rook and the rook jumps over to the square next to the king. White goes from e1 to g1 and the rook from h1 to f1. Notation: **O-O**.
- **Queenside castling (long)**: the king moves two squares toward the a-file rook, and the rook lands next to it. White goes from e1 to c1 and the rook from a1 to d1. Notation: **O-O-O**.

On a real board you move the **king first**, two squares, and then the rook. In most online games you simply move the king two squares and the rook jumps by itself.

## The conditions for castling

You can castle only if **all** of the following are true:

1. The **king has not moved** earlier in the game.
2. The **rook you castle with has not moved**.
3. All squares between the king and that rook are **empty**.
4. The king is **not in check**.
5. The king does **not pass through or land on an attacked square**.

If one of these is not true, castling is not allowed *at the moment* or *for the rest of the game*. Conditions 1 and 2 are permanent, while 3, 4 and 5 are only checked when you want to castle.

## Examples where castling is illegal

### Through an attacked square

{{img:ch-castle-through|The black rook attacks f1, so the White king may not castle kingside; castling queenside is still legal.}}

In this position the black rook on f8 controls the f-file, so f1 is attacked. The white king would have to pass over f1 to reach g1, so **kingside castling is illegal**. The king can still castle on the queenside, because d1 and c1 are not attacked.

### Out of check

If your king is in check, you cannot castle, even if all other conditions are satisfied. You must respond to the check by moving the king, blocking or capturing.

### After moving the king or rook

If you moved the king earlier, you cannot castle on either side, even if you moved it back. If you moved only the h-rook, you can still castle queenside with the a-rook, and the other way round.

## Things that do *not* stop castling

- **An attacked rook.** Only the king's squares matter.
- **An attacked b1 square** (when castling queenside). The rook may pass over b1 even if it is attacked, because only the squares the king crosses are checked.
- **A previous check** that you already escaped, as long as the king has not moved.

## Why castle?

- **King safety**: the king ends up behind a wall of pawns instead of the open centre.
- **Rook activity**: the rook leaves the corner and takes part in the game.
- **Tempo**: you do two useful things in one move.

## When not to castle

Castling is not always right. Consider delaying it when the opponent's pieces are aimed at the side where you want to castle, or when a pawn storm against that side is coming. A common choice is to castle toward the side with fewer enemy attackers.

## Common mistakes

- Trying to castle through check (the game will not let you).
- Moving the rook first and then trying to castle.
- Moving the king early for no reason and losing the right to castle.
- Castling into a ready-made attack; look at where the opponent's pieces are aimed.
