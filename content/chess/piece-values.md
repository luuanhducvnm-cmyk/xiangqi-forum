Chess piece values are a **rule of thumb** for judging trades. They are not part of the rules, and a stronger position often beats a bigger point count, but they are a good first test before you capture or let your opponent capture something.

## The standard values

| Piece | Value | How to remember it |
|---|---|---|
| [Pawn](/chess/pieces/) | **1** | The unit everything else is measured in |
| [Knight](/chess/pieces/) | **3** | Jumps, but is slow |
| [Bishop](/chess/pieces/) | **3** | Long-range, but limited to one colour of square |
| [Rook](/chess/pieces/) | **5** | Powerful on open files and ranks |
| [Queen](/chess/pieces/) | **9** | Combines the rook and bishop; about a rook plus a bishop plus a pawn |
| King | — | Cannot be traded; it matters in the endgame |

Each player starts with 8 pawns (8 points), 2 knights (6), 2 bishops (6), 2 rooks (10) and a queen (9): **39 points** of material in total.

## Simple trade rules

- **Equal trades are fine.** Knight for bishop, rook for rook, queen for queen: no gain or loss in points.
- **Never give a bigger piece for a smaller one** without a reason. Trading a rook (5) for a bishop (3) is called "losing the exchange".
- **Two minor pieces for a rook and a pawn** (6 for 6) is roughly equal in points, but the minor pieces are often stronger in the middlegame.
- **Three minor pieces for a queen** (9 for 9) is about equal; which side is better depends on the position.
- **When ahead, trade pieces** (especially queens) to reduce the other side's counterplay. When behind, avoid trades and look for tactics.

## The bishop pair and other bonuses

Experienced players adjust the numbers a little:

1. **The bishop pair:** two bishops together cover both colours of square and are usually worth a bit more than a bishop and a knight, especially in open positions.
2. **Passed pawns:** a pawn with no enemy pawn in front or on the adjacent files is worth much more as it advances, and a pawn one step from promotion can be worth a rook or more.
3. **Doubled and isolated pawns:** pawns on the same file, or with no friendly pawn on the neighbouring files, are worth less than 1.
4. **Rooks gain value in endgames,** when files are open. **Knights lose value** when the board is open and the pawns have disappeared.

## When the points lie

A few common situations where the count misleads:

- **A king under attack.** A side that is behind in material but has a forced [checkmate](/chess/checkmates/) is winning, whatever the points say.
- **A trapped piece.** A bishop that has no safe squares is worth less than 3.
- **Hanging a piece for tempo.** A quick attack can win more material than it sacrifices ([tactics](/chess/tactics/)).
- **Endgames with few pieces.** A lone knight or bishop with a king can never checkmate, so insufficient material can turn an extra "point" into a draw.

## How to use values in your games

1. Before every capture, count what you win and what you lose if the opponent recaptures.
2. Look at what the capture changes in the position, not only the points.
3. In an endgame, ask whether you have enough material to checkmate ([endgames](/chess/endgames/)).
