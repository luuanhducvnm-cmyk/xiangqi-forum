Stalemate is one of the most surprising ways a chess game can end. The player to move has no legal move, but their king is not in check, and the game is a **draw**.

## The rule

A game is stalemated when **both** of these are true:

1. It is a player's turn and they have **no legal move** with any piece.
2. Their king is **not in check**.

If the king were in check, it would be [checkmate](/chess/checkmates/) instead. The result is a draw: neither side wins.

## Why stalemate matters

- **It saves lost positions.** A player with only a king left can aim for stalemate, and a careless opponent may fall into it.
- **It can ruin a win.** The most common case is the endgame with a king and queen against a king, where the attacker takes away every square without noticing that it gives no check.
- **It does not happen early.** In the middlegame a player almost always has some legal move, so stalemate is mostly an endgame idea.

## How to avoid stalemating your opponent

When you are winning, before you play a move that restricts the enemy king, ask:

1. **Is my opponent in check after this move?** If yes, it is not stalemate.
2. **Does my opponent have any legal move?** Count the king's free squares and any other piece that can move.
3. **Can I leave one free square on purpose** and then close in on the next move?

Practise the method in the [endgame principles](/chess/endgames/): the "box" technique for king and queen against king always keeps a legal move for the lone king until the final check.

## How to use stalemate when you are losing

- **Keep a pawn or piece you can sacrifice.** If your opponent has just a king and queen, run your king towards a corner and see whether the winning side makes a mistake.
- **Do not hope for it too early.** Stalemate tricks work only when the opponent is careless, and a strong player will not fall for them.
