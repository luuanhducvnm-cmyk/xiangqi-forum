The **Sicilian Defence** begins **1.e4 c5**. Instead of copying White with 1...e5, Black attacks the centre from the side. The position becomes **unbalanced**: White usually gets more space and a kingside attack, Black gets the half-open c-file and queenside counterplay. **All diagrams below were verified with the Chess 3D rules engine.**

## The Open Sicilian

The main line is **2.Nf3 d6 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nf6**. White has traded the d-pawn for Black's c-pawn, giving White a central pawn on e4 and a knight on d4.

{{img:ln-sic-open|Open Sicilian after 4...Nf6: White has the centre, Black the c-file.}}

## The Najdorf Variation

After **5.Nc3 a6** Black plays the famous Najdorf move. The pawn on a6 prevents White's pieces from jumping to b5 and prepares ...e5 or ...b5.

{{img:ln-najdorf|The Najdorf Sicilian: 5...a6.}}

## Typical plans

- **White:** attack on the kingside with f3 or f4, g4 and pieces; use the extra space in the centre.
- **Black:** pressure the queenside and the c-file, and strike at the centre with ...d5 or ...e5 when ready.
- Both sides must be ready to castle on opposite sides, which leads to races: whoever attacks faster wins.

## Why people choose the Sicilian

Because both sides have winning chances. A draw is less likely than in more symmetrical openings, so the Sicilian is popular when a player needs to win as Black. The price is that you must know typical tactics and know your pawn structure. Practise tactics with our [mate-in-3 puzzles](/chess/puzzles/mate-in-3/).

## Mistakes to avoid

1. Moving pawns on the kingside when your king has not castled yet.
2. Taking material on the queenside while your pieces are not developed.
3. Forgetting that the d4 and e4 squares decide the middlegame.
